Admission Watch

A new academic year has begun! Here’s a sampling of the Class of 2030 as it arrived on campus.

Albany’s First-Year Class Up by 20 Percent. In August, the U. of Albany, founded in 1844 and a member of the State U. of New York (SUNY), matriculated more than 3,500 students into its Class of 2030, 20 percent more than last year. They were selected from more than 36,000 applicants.

Albany enrolls a total of more than 18,000 students from across New York state, including Albany, New York City, Long Island, 40 states and 35 countries. Earlier this year, UAlbany announced a guaranteed admission program for Albany H. S. students and plans to expand the program to other high schools.

Among UAlbany’s top programs are public affairs and public policy, criminal justice, public health, business, information science, atmospheric and environmental sciences.

Alleghany Also Up 20 Percent. Alleghany C., founded in 1815 and located 90 miles north of Pittsburgh, matriculated 375 first-year students and 22 transfers in late August, 20 percent more than last year, according to the Meadville Tribune. They arrived from 27 states and Washington, D.C., with about half coming from Pennsylvania. International students represent 29 countries.

About half graduated high school with above a 3.6 unweighted grade point average (GPA), and 40 posted 4.0s. For students who reported a class rank, 55 percent placed among the top quarter of their high school graduating class, the paper reported.

The class's top five most common choices for majors were: business, biology, psychology, undecided and environmental science and sustainability.

"Allegheny is honored to welcome its largest class in the last five years," Jennifer Winge, vice president for enrollment management, told the paper. "We're thrilled to have formally minted the Class of 2030 and our transfer students as Gators and can't wait to see how their contributions enrich our campus."

Air Force’s I-Day. The 1,130 cadets of the incoming Class of 2030 at the United States Air Force Academy in Colorado, who will become officers in the Air Force and Space Force, were selected from 10,970 applicants. They include new cadets from 17 countries across Africa, Asia, Europe and South America, such as Ghana, Kosovo, Taiwan, Turkey, Egypt, Indonesia, Ecuador, Senegal, Bangladesh, Cameroon, Jordan, South Korea, the Maldives, Morocco, Pakistan, Thailand and Togo.

June 24th was their first day, In-processing Day or I-Day, for the 47-month program designed to prepare them for military leadership and service. "The young men and women who showed up today have taken their first steps toward becoming the warfighters and leaders of character the Air Force and Space Force needs. I-Day is where that transformation begins," Commandant of Cadets Col. Brandon J. Tellez told KRDO News.

"This year, we have admitted one of our most competitive classes in Academy history, with composite scores coming in seven percent higher than previous averages," added Col. Candice L. Pipes, the Academy's director of admissions. "These young men and women understand the rigor and the profound service obligation they are undertaking and actively chose to answer that call."

Augustana Speaks 25 Languages. In August, Augustana C. in Rock Island, Illinois welcomed 765 new students from 28 states and 36 countries. The incoming class includes 678 first-year students, 82 transfer students and a total of five non-degree and exchange students, with 25 languages spoken on campus. Augustana received more than 8,600 applications for the incoming class.

Kent Barnds, executive vice president for strategy and innovation, told the River Cities’ Reader that relationships, mentorship and guidance will be central to helping members of the Class of 2030 discover who they are and what they want to do with their lives. “Each of our incoming students brings a unique perspective to Augustana,” Barnds said. “Our responsibility now is to help each of them find a strong sense of belonging and pursue opportunities that will prepare them for meaningful lives and careers.”

The average high school GPA for the Class of 2030 is 3.42. A total of 67 students are members of the Austin E Knowlton Honors Program and 21 students are members of the Brenda Barnes Leadership Academy.

A total of 281 students receive either the Dean’s Scholarship or Presidential Scholarship, which can total up to $128,000 over four years, while 54 students receive music scholarships. Additionally, 179 students receive grants through Augustana Possible, a financial aid program that meets up to 100 percent of a qualifying student’s demonstrated financial need, determined by the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid). Another 287 students receive Federal Pell Grants, “further reinforcing Augustana’s commitment to making a high-quality liberal arts education accessible to students from all socioeconomic backgrounds.”

The top ten programs identified by the entering cohort include biology and pre-health programs, business administration, psychology and neuroscience, education, engineering, kinesiology, computer science, accounting and political science.

Augustana’s admissions staff team visited 333 U.S. high schools and spent 104 days abroad, visiting nineteen countries.

Bowdoin’s 120 Year Promise. Bowdoin C. in Maine attracted 14,727 applicants for its Class of 2030, admitted 962 and enrolled 557 of them. About 52 percent are women and 68 percent hail from outside New England, while 34 percent are students of color and 19 percent are first-generation college students. They traveled from 14 states, and 12 percent are from international destinations. Bowdoin has been test-optional for more than 50 years and 46 percent of new students did not submit a standardized test score.

Bowdoin promises prospective students, in the words of William DeWitte Hyde, its president in 1906, that they will: “be at home in all lands and all ages; count Nature a familiar acquaintance, and Art an intimate friend; gain a standard for the appreciation of others' work and the criticism of your own; carry the keys of the world's library in your pocket, and feel its resources behind you in whatever task you undertake; make hosts of friends... who are to be leaders in all walks of life; lose yourself in generous enthusiasms and cooperate with others for common ends;” and that these will be “the best four years of your life.”

Bucknell’s Largest Class. Bucknell U. in Pennsylvania is celebrated its 181st year by matriculating its Class of 2030, the largest in school history, 1,067 new students, selected from among 10,661 applicants. Total school size is a record 4,054 students, including 3,932 degree-seeking undergraduates, 89 non-degree undergraduates and 33 graduate students.

According to the university, students in the new class hail from 38 states, the District of Columbia and 51 countries and regions. The class posted an average high school GPA of 3.6, slightly higher than last year's 3.58.

"What stands out about the Class of 2030 is the extraordinary breadth of experiences they bring to Bucknell," said vice president of enrollment management & dean of admissions Kevin Mathes. "They arrive as researchers, entrepreneurs, artists, athletes, community leaders and volunteers, and many have already made a meaningful impact in their schools and communities. We're excited to see what they'll accomplish when they bring those experiences together here at Bucknell."

The new class includes students who conducted scholarship in fields ranging from cardiovascular medicine and adolescent development to air pollution, economics, mathematical modeling and brain-computer interfaces. They have published research in the Journal of Emerging Investigators and Reviews in Cardiovascular Medicine.

One student's original work was displayed at the Museum of Modern Art, while another created more than 40 original pixel-animation videos and 200 individual artworks. The class also includes a student who launched a professional photography business in high school, a screenwriter developing an original television series and accomplished dancers, musicians and theatre performers.

Athletically, the class includes a Baseball Canada national champion, a nationally ranked golfer, a national club champion and Junior Olympian in field hockey and a tennis player with a top 20 national doubles ranking.

Enrollment Trends

Common App 2026 Totals. About 9.4 million applications to institutions of higher learning were submitted through the Common Application during the 2025-26 application cycle. That was a 2.1 million increase compared to the 2021-22 application cycle. The 9.4 million applications were received from 1.4 million individuals, meaning prospective undergraduate students still are, on average, submitting applications to six or seven schools.

Florida Bans Undocumented Students. In July, Florida's State Board of Education voted 6-1 to bar undocumented immigrants from admission to the state's public colleges, including to adult education programs, according to The Orlando Sentinel. As of 2023, Florida was home to approximately 50,000 undocumented immigrant students.

Howard Uproar. In July, Howard U. in Washington D.C. reportedly shocked 502 students by sending emails informing them that they had been unenrolled from their classes and the university due to unpaid balances. They hadn’t met the 50 percent tuition payment requirement by the deadline set on July 10. As a result, they were told their place couldn’t be held any longer and would not be accepted for the Fall 2026 semester.

Howard noted, “We understand that the enrollment update that was issued yesterday is difficult and disappointing for students and their families. Howard University provided multiple detailed communications throughout March, April, May, June, and July to first-time-in-college students and families on steps needed to secure enrollment for the Fall 2026 semester. … As Howard finalizes enrollment for the Fall 2026 semester, which begins August 17, confirming enrollment is essential to planning academic programs, housing, financial aid, and student support services,” according to BlackAmericaweb.com.

But after widespread uproar, Howard reinstated about 200 of the 502 incoming students, according to The Washington Post. Laverne Mickens, a scholarship coach who worked with affected Howard students, told the Post, “I really think that Howard thought these kids weren’t coming. They weren’t serious.”

Other state politicians joined in the ruckus.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul announced that affected New Yorkers could seek admission via expedited late admissions review at City University of New York and State University of New York campuses. Maryland Governor Wes Moore announced that his administration will “provide expedited admission support to University System of Maryland schools, explore financial aid options, and provide an $800 credit for impacted students.” The U. of the District of Columbia, Washington D.C.’s only public university and another HBCU, extended its fall admissions deadline by one week to August 7 to accommodate affected Howard students.

“Maxxing” Versus Authenticity. “‘Maxxing.’ It’s all the rage in 2026—looksmaxxing, protein maxxing, now even tan maxxing.” According to an August 1 report in Forbes, many college applicants are yielding to cultural trends in fitness and nutrition that lead them to “maxxing” their identity on their college applications. “Applicants mistakenly believe that they need to do everything and be everything to be admitted to college.”

Instead, admissions officers who spoke with Forbes said, “The reality is that college admission can be joyful, simple, and fulfilling—a celebration of one’s strengths and values as they are, and not how society tells them they should be.’” Forbes noted, “In truth, admission professionals are not seeking flawless, excessive resumes from superhuman applicants. In fact, these applications often land as inauthentic and induce eyerolling from those reviewing them. Rather, colleges are hoping to admit students who have found meaning in what they do and are ready to engage and contribute to a vibrant campus community.”

Midwest Demographic Cliff. According to the Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education, Ohio graduated 143,303 high school students in 2025. It projects 134,175 in 2030, 134,895 in 2035 and 124,461 in 2041. That means the 2030 graduating class will measure 6.4 percent below the 2025 peak and the 2041 class 13.1 percent below that.

Ohio’s projected decline is smaller than the 16 percent decrease forecast for the Midwest from 2023 through 2041. Michigan is projected to lose 20 percent of its high school graduates and Illinois 32 percent over that period.

Northwestern Ends Posse Partnership. After 13 years, Northwestern U. ended its partnership with the Posse Foundation, a national nonprofit organization that partners with colleges across the country to provide full-tuition scholarships to students from diverse backgrounds who exhibit leadership in their communities.

“Posse’s mission is to diversify the pool from which top universities recruit young leaders, build welcoming environments at these institutions for students of all backgrounds and ensure that the selected scholars graduate,” the program declares.

NU notified Posse Scholars and affiliated faculty in late June that it will be ending its partnership with The Posse Foundation, making the incoming Class of 2030 cohort the program’s last.

In a July 15 statement to The Daily Northwestern, a university spokesperson wrote that NU “regularly reviews how it uses its resources to support its academic mission. By phasing out its Posse program, Northwestern will be able to reallocate those funds to further support its commitments to access, opportunity and belonging regardless of students’ hometown or financial means.”

Those close to the decision said that NU wants to focus on programs specifically serving first-generation and low-income students, as opposed to Posse’s merit-based scholarships. It is unclear whether the move was related to recent negotiations with the Trump administration over DEI issues.

New Strict Deadlines for Visa Holders. The U.S. Department of Homeland Security has replaced the Duration of Status system with a strict four-year admission limit for F-1 and J-1 visa holders.

As a result, many universities advised international students to return to campus before September 15, when the new immigration rule takes effect. Students already in the U.S. under the old system by September 15 will retain limited protections, but these cannot extend beyond November 14, 2030.

The new rule requires F-1 student visa holders, J-1 exchange visitors to cap their stay at a maximum of four years. The rule is set to take effect on September 15, 2026. The longstanding Duration of Status (D/S) framework allowed international students to remain in the US for as long as they maintained their academic standing.

"This is a self-inflicted wound on U.S. competitiveness” Khanderao Kand, chief of policy and strategy at the Foundation for India and Indian Diaspora Studies (FIIDS) told NDTV. “A 4-year cap misaligns with how modern degrees actually work, median time to a bachelor's is about 52 months and to a PhD about 5.7 years, while USCIS is already sitting on more than 11 million cases with roughly year-long processing times. Students will be forced out mid-program or mid-research, labs will lose critical talent, and America will hand its innovation pipeline to competitors."

Financial Matters

California’s FAFSA Record. The California Student Aid Commission reported that the high school Class of 2026 posted a 70 percent completion rate for financial aid applications, either the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) or the California Dream Act Application (CADAA), up 17 percentage points from 53 percent in the 2021–22 academic year.

“High School Seniors, you made history this year,” exclaimed Daisy Gonzales, executive director of the commission. “This milestone is a testament to the hard work of our students, families, educators and community partners across the state.”

College Sports Programs Lose Money and Students Pay. According to a recent Government Accountability Office (GAO) report, 94 percent of Division I athletics programs (330 of 352 colleges) lost money in the 2023-24 academic year. Division I colleges spent $20.8 billion on sports while generating $13.1 billion. The median school's gap was $20.6 million.

To make up for the difference, colleges transferred $7.2 billion to athletics from tuition, student fees and other unrestricted sources, which indirectly included federal student aid. GAO estimates that added up to about $8,500 per undergraduate over a four-year degree, ranging from $3,200 at Power conference schools to $10,800 at Football Championship Subdivision schools. “Every student pays, not just athletes, and families don't really see it broken out when they calculate the real cost of college,” noted MSN. It is another reason why tuition keeps rising faster than inflation.

Student Loan Defaults Hit New Record. Defaults on federal student loans are reaching record levels. Since repayments came due again following the COVID-19 pandemic pause, about 9.5 million people, 20 percent of student loan borrowers, are in default, up from 5.3 million, according to data from the Office of Federal Student Aid.

Nearly $233.3 billion out of $1.7 trillion in federally backed student loans nationwide are in default. That means they are more than nine months behind on their payments. That could lead to serious consequences for the default borrowers, including garnished wages or Social Security payments.

Out of the schools in the top quarter for nonpayment rates, 76 percent were for-profit schools.

Mississippi has the nation's highest default rate, 28.3 percent. Others near the top include Louisiana, Alabama, West Virginia, Oklahoma, Georgia, South Carolina and Texas. Rounding out the 15 states with the highest default rates are Alaska, Arizona, Ohio, Indiana, Michigan, New Mexico and Nevada.

"Folks are struggling to make ends meet and cover all the rising costs of everything else,” Aissa Canchola Bañez, policy director for the advocacy group Protect Borrowers, told CBS News Money Watch. “The growing student loan bills are making things worse and folks are falling behind."

AI Requirements. According to the Los Angeles Times, “Schools like VCU are starting up AI minors targeting students outside computer science. There’s a new AI graduation requirement at Purdue University. In freshman writing classes, Harvard University is teaching students how large language models work, plus exploring AI topics such as copyright and disinformation. And Ohio State University has an AI fluency requirement that includes hands-on workshops. Meanwhile, AI-focused undergraduate and even graduate degrees are proliferating.”

Massachusetts’s Three-Year Degrees. According to WBUR, Boston U.’s public radio station, “Some Massachusetts colleges will offer reduced-credit, three-year bachelor's degrees beginning in Fall of 2027, after the state's Board of Higher Education approved pilot programs at two local universities back in June. Officials say the accelerated degrees, which require around 90 credits compared to the usual 120, could save students $10,000 to $20,000 in a time when more young adults are questioning the value of education.”

Massachusetts joins a growing number of states that have launched three-year bachelor's degree programs, including Indiana, Ohio, Maine and Utah.

"Students are changing," Massachusetts Commissioner of Higher Education Noé Ortega told Morning Edition. "We are no longer just seeing 18-to- 24-year-olds coming into our institutions of higher education. We have more students who are first generation, more students who are returning back to college after some time." Ortega said many of these students "want to go through the program faster" so “they can begin making money and provide for their families faster.”

Michigan Drops First Semester Grades. Next year, the U. of Michigan’s C. of Literature, Science and the Arts (LSA) will pilot a program that will “cover grades” from first-semester freshmen to “curb the mental health crisis unfolding among college-aged individuals.” Students will receive grades that only professors and advisors see and that will appear as “P” pass or “NC” No Credit on their transcripts.

“Our goal is to help first-year students acclimate to the demands of college and allow intrinsic motivations to guide personally meaningful academic journeys, core elements of a liberal arts and sciences education,” the Michigan website states.

Other schools such as MIT and Cal Tech are doing the same. Johns Hopkins U. tried the approach for four decades but ended it in 2017. Its faculty said covered grades too often delayed students from developing study skills and adapting to college-level work.

Texas Adds 24 Programs. According to The Daily Texan, UT-Austin has added 24 new programs to its 2026-28 course catalogue. These include three new bachelor’s degrees in neuroscience and computer science, linguistics and computer science and history and computer science. Also, new majors in materials science and engineering, strategy and statecraft have been added. Its School of Civic Leadership’s great books program “will examine texts deemed foundational to Western civilization.”

The Moody College of Communication “will add advertising and radio-television-film minors to its catalog, along with a new minor in digital game industries. The College of Fine Arts will double its catalog of minors with new offerings for dance, theatre and music,” the student paper reported.

Among several other new minors, the LBJ School of Affairs “will open minor programs for international development and national security. Meanwhile, the College of Natural Sciences will receive two new minors in the form of child and family health and human development and family sciences.”

Wisconsin’s B.A in Public Affairs. Beginning this fall, the U. of Wisconsin-Madison’s LaFollette School of Public Affairs offers a B.A. degree in public affairs. For the past 40 years, only graduate degrees and certificates were offered. The undergraduate degree will open pathways to careers in government, nonprofits, consulting, advocacy, business, graduate study and law. Its curriculum focuses on civil dialogue, working across differences and finding common ground in an age of polarization.

“Interest in public policy is skyrocketing,” Lafollette director Susan Webb Yackee told On Wisconsin. “Students increasingly view data, dialogue and diverse perspectives as key to building better solutions.”

UW-Madison also earned a gold rating from the Sustainability Tracking, Assessment, and Rating System (STARS.) Some 85 of its departments offer sustainability courses, 57 of which offer a major or certificate that requires sustainability knowledge. UW also has high levels of student involvement with more than 50 registered student organizations and campus events, including an award-winning Earth Fest, On Wisconsin reports.

First SAT. “On June 23, 1926, more than 8,000 high school students sat down to take the first version of what was then called the Scholastic Aptitude Test, or the SAT,” noted a June 26, 2026, article in the Smithsonian Magazine. “The teens spent roughly an hour and a half attempting to answer 315 questions about word definitions, analogies, math and more in the hopes that their score would help them gain admission to top colleges. In just a few years, Harvard even began to use the test to find talented students beyond those at prestigious boarding schools on the East Coast.” A century later, more than half of the two million graduating high school students take the SAT each year. See: www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/100-years-ago-students-across-the-us-took-the-first-sat-today-relatively-few-colleges-require-the-test-where-is-it-headed-180988997/

ETS Buys ACT. In July, ETS, the Education Testing Service, a Princeton, New Jersey, nonprofit that runs the GRE (graduate school admissions exam) and the TOEFL (English-language proficiency test), acquired ACT, one of the nation's largest college admissions testing organizations. College Board owns the SAT.

"Every student deserves a strong education, a fair shot at college and a path to a good job," ETS CEO Amit Sevak said in a statement. "Together with ACT, we're determined to serve students and parents along with educators and states by expanding access to education and job opportunities across America."
